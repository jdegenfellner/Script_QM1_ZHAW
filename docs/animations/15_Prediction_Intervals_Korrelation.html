Correlation and prediction intervals

X and Y are jointly normal, each with mean 0 and SD 1, with the correlation you choose. Move the orange line to a value of X. The grey interval is where a new Y falls if you know nothing about X, the orange interval is where it falls if you know X. How much shorter is the orange one?

The numbers. Without X: mean ± z · SD of Y, with z = 1.96 for 95 %. Given X = x: the mean of Y moves to ρ · x, and the SD shrinks from 1 to √(1 − ρ²), so the interval is ρ · x ± z · √(1 − ρ²). Its length is √(1 − ρ²) times the original length, i.e. it is shorter by 1 − √(1 − ρ²).

Why a high correlation helps so little for one person. The curve on the right is flat at the beginning: ρ = 0.5 shortens the interval by only 13 %, ρ = 0.7 by 29 %, ρ = 0.9 by 56 %. The position of X changes where the interval lies, but not how long it is. The length depends on ρ alone.

What is not included. The intervals use the true ρ, means and SDs. In practice these are estimated, so a prediction interval from data is a bit wider (the estimation error of the regression line comes on top). Normality of X and Y is assumed.
